A burst pipe is a split or blown-out joint in a water supply line, usually from freezing, corrosion, age or high pressure. The rupture can be a hairline pinhole or a foot-long tear. Either way, the fix starts with controlling the water and then finding what failed and why.

Burst pipe emergency: shut it down in this order

Speed matters more than neatness. These steps limit the damage before help arrives.

  1. Close the main shutoff valve. It is usually near where the water line enters the house, often in the basement, a utility room or a crawlspace, and is a wheel handle or a lever.
  2. Open the lowest faucet in the house and the highest one to drain what is left in the pipes.
  3. Switch off power to wet areas at the breaker, but only if you can reach the panel without stepping in water. If you cannot, stay out and call us and your electric utility.
  4. Turn off the water heater at its power or gas control so it does not run dry, then move valuables and rugs out of the puddle.
  5. Take photos and video of the damage before you clean anything up.

Finding your main shutoff before you need it

In most homes the main valve sits on the wall facing the street, close to the meter or the point where the line comes through the foundation. Slab homes sometimes put it in a closet or garage. If you have a public meter, a second valve may sit in a curb box outside, and your utility can tell you whether you may operate it.

Walk to yours now and turn it fully closed, then open it again. A valve that has not moved in years may be stuck or drip at the stem. Tag it with a bright strip of tape so anyone in the house can find it, and tell us if it will not close.

Frozen pipes: thaw them without making it worse

NWS guidance says to turn on the faucet first, then warm the pipe with a space heater, a heating pad, a hair dryer or hot water on a cloth. Never use an open flame, and call a plumber if the problem continues. Pipes on exterior walls, in basements, in attics and under kitchen cabinets freeze most often.

Start thawing at the faucet end and work toward the blockage. If a pipe is already cracked, thawing it releases the water, so keep the main valve nearby. If you cannot find where the pipe is frozen, or the ice is behind a wall, stop and call.

Long outages: how Simsbury storms put pipes at risk

The October 2011 snowstorm, called Storm Alfred, struck Connecticut on October 29 with about 830,000 customers without power statewide, and some homes stayed dark for nearly two weeks. The Capitol Region hazard plan says it wreaked by far the most havoc on Simsbury. A house without heat during a cold snap can drop below freezing quickly, so do not wait to see what happens.

Tropical Storm Isaias in August 2020 left about 3,000 Eversource customers in Simsbury without power on the August 5 update, and about 8 were still out by August 11. That was summer, so freezing was not the issue, but it shows how long outages can run. Plan for several days, not several hours.

A freeze-prevention routine for a long outage

Shut the main and drain the system if the house will go cold, since pipes without water cannot burst. Open the lowest and highest faucets, flush toilets once, and leave drain traps with a splash of non-toxic antifreeze if you have it.

While the house still holds some warmth, NWS advice is to let faucets drip and open cabinet doors under sinks so warmer air reaches the pipes. NWS also says to keep the heat at 55 degrees or higher when you are away, which is a useful floor to aim for. Close off an unheated room and gather in the warmest part of the house. For a longer outage, a plumber can fully drain the system and the water heater.

How we find the failure and repair it

Once the water is off, the repair depends on the material and how much of the line is damaged.

Spot repair

  • Cut out the split section and install a short new piece.
  • Copper uses a soldered joint or a press fitting that crimps without a flame.
  • PEX uses crimp or expansion fittings and tolerates some freezing better than rigid pipe.
  • Best for one failure in otherwise sound piping.

Longer run or reroute

  • Replace a full length of pipe when several spots have failed.
  • Reroute out of a cold outside wall if the same line freezes every winter.
  • More work, but it fixes the cause and not just the symptom.
  • If pinholes keep appearing, a repipe may beat repeated patches.

Drying out and documenting for insurance

The EPA says wet materials should be dried within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold. Start documenting at the same time.

  • Photograph the burst, the water line on walls and every damaged item before you move it.
  • Keep the failed pipe section if the plumber removes it, since an adjuster may ask to see it.
  • Ask the plumber for a written note of what failed, what was repaired and the date.
  • Run fans and a dehumidifier, and pull up wet carpet and pad quickly.
  • Call your insurer and ask what they require. Standard homeowners policies generally cover damage from frozen and burst pipes, but sewer and drain backups usually need a separate endorsement.

Keeping it from happening again

  • Insulate pipes in exterior walls, attics, garages and crawlspaces, and seal cracks near water lines with caulk.
  • Open cabinet doors on cold nights and let a faucet drip on the coldest lines.
  • Set the heat no lower than 55 degrees if you are away, and the Insurance Information Institute suggests 65 or higher at home.
  • Disconnect garden hoses and shut off outdoor spigots before the first hard freeze.
  • If a line freezes more than once, ask about moving it or rerouting it rather than waiting for the next split.

Where this repair stops

We repair pipes. We do not handle the full drying and restoration work after a flood, which is a separate contractor. A burst in the street or on the utility side of the meter is for the utility.
