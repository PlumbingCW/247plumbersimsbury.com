A sump pump sits in a pit, usually the lowest spot in the basement or crawlspace, and pushes groundwater out through a discharge line before it reaches the floor. The system is a pump, a float switch, a check valve and a pipe outside. Any one of those failing leaves you with a pit that fills and a basement that follows.

Basement flooding right now: electrical safety first

Before you touch anything

Water and electricity do not mix, and a flooded basement often has outlets, a panel or a water heater at floor level.

  • Do not stand in water to unplug the pump or operate a breaker or fuse box. A municipal utility district advises never to operate or disconnect electrical equipment or a main fuse box while standing in water, and to call the electric utility if power must be shut off.
  • If the panel is dry and you can reach it without stepping in water, switch off power to the basement circuits.
  • Close the main water valve if a pipe, not groundwater, is the source.
  • Move valuables, boxes and electronics upstairs and off the floor.
  • Call the 24/7 line and tell us whether the pump is humming, silent or running without moving water.

Signs the pump is failing

Runs but moves little water

A loud motor with a thin flow often means a jammed impeller, a clogged intake or a failing motor.

Never shuts off

A float switch stuck up or tangled on the pit wall keeps the motor running until it burns out.

Won't start

A tripped GFCI, a dead switch or a seized motor. Always check the outlet before assuming the pump is bad.

Short cycling or loud thumps

A bad check valve lets the discharge pipe drain back into the pit. The pump restarts constantly and the hammer shakes the pipe.

Old and untested

Pumps wear out. If yours has never been tested, treat it as unproven until it is.

How we diagnose a pump problem

  • Pour water into the pit and watch the float lift, the pump start and the water leave the discharge line.
  • Check the electrical circuit, GFCI and cord for damage.
  • Look at the check valve for backflow and the discharge pipe for kinks or freezing.
  • Measure the pit and compare it to the pump's capacity and the amount of water it must handle.
  • Test the backup, including battery charge and whether it triggers at the right level.

Pit, discharge line and check valve details

A pit that is too small makes the pump start and stop constantly, which wears it out fast. A pit that is deep and wide enough gives the pump time to rest between cycles. We size it to the water you actually get, not a rule of thumb.

The discharge line should carry water well away from the foundation and not toward the neighbor's yard or onto the septic field. The EPA says to keep roof drains and sump pumps away from a drainfield, and to make sure the ground slopes away from the building. Ask the Simsbury building department if you are unsure where the line can go.

A check valve in the discharge line stops water from running back into the pit. They fail quietly and are a small part to swap, so test them every time you service the pump.

A testing routine that takes five minutes

The UConn Extension advises checking that your sump pump works before heavy rain. Do it every season and before a forecast storm.

  • Pour a bucket of water into the pit and watch the pump start, empty it and shut off.
  • Look at the outside end of the discharge line for ice, debris or crushed pipe.
  • Unplug the primary pump and make sure the backup starts.
  • Check the battery charge and replace it on the maker's schedule.
  • Keep the pit cover on and clear of dirt, stones and debris.

What a pump cannot do

A sump pump manages groundwater at the pit. It does not seal cracks, stop seepage through walls or fix a bad grade outside. For that, see waterproofing options and have a foundation specialist look at structural cracks.
