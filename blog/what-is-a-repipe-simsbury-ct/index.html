What Is a Repipe? A Guide for Simsbury, CT Homeowners

Written by the Simsbury 24/7 Plumbing team · Published October 15, 2026 · 9 min read

A repipe replaces the supply pipes that carry water to your fixtures. Here is what it is, when a house needs one, which materials are used and how to judge a quote before you agree to open walls.

A repipe is the replacement of the water supply piping inside a house, the lines that run from where water enters the building to every tap, tub and appliance. It does not usually include the drain and sewer pipes, which carry waste away. Most repipes happen because the old supply pipes are leaking, corroding or restricting flow.

Repiping is a big decision, and homeowners in Bloomfield and Windsor weigh the same questions. We do not publish prices, because they depend on the size of the house, the material and how much wall has to be opened.

Whole-house or partial: what a repipe can cover

A whole-house repipe replaces the supply lines to every fixture, from the main shutoff to the water heater and out to each bathroom, kitchen and laundry. A partial repipe replaces one section, such as a bathroom branch or the lines in a basement ceiling. That is sensible when the problem is limited and the rest of the pipe is sound.

A leak in one section often means the rest is the same age and condition. That is why a repipe comes up after the second or third repair on the same line. The pattern, not a single leak, tells you the system is wearing out.

Pinholes, brown water and weak flow: signs you may need one

  • Pinhole leaks in copper that keep reappearing in new places, with green crust, damp drywall or a ceiling stain.
  • Brown, yellow or rusty water from the taps, especially after the house has sat unused for hours.
  • Low pressure at several fixtures, not just one. EPA says fixtures work best between 45 and 60 psi, and much lower than 45 can signal a leak.
  • Repeated repairs on the same line, or a plumber telling you the pipe is brittle, scaled inside or corroded at the joints.
  • A water bill that rises with no change in use, or a meter that moves with all fixtures off.

Before you commit: confirm that it is the pipes

Low pressure and discolored water do not always mean failing pipes. A partly closed main valve, a clogged aerator or a failing pressure regulator can cut flow, and sediment from a water heater can discolor hot water only. A technician should test pressure with a gauge on a hose bib.

If you suspect a leak but cannot find it, leak detection with listening gear, thermal imaging or pressure tests pinpoints the spot, and it may show one repair is enough. EPA suggests a meter test, reading the meter before and after two hours with no water used.

What a repipe is like in an occupied home

Most repipes happen with the family still living there.

1

Plan the route

The plumber walks the house, marks where lines will run and decides where small access holes in drywall or ceilings are needed.

2

Protect and open

Floors and furniture are covered, and holes are cut in drywall at the key points. Expect dust, noise and some rooms off limits.

3

Run the new lines

New pipe is run to each fixture and connected to the shutoffs, the water heater and the supply. Water is off for stretches, so ask how they handle it.

4

Test and restore

The system is pressure tested, the old lines are disconnected and the access holes are patched, unless the quote says someone else does the drywall and paint.

How to compare repipe quotes

The details decide whether two quotes are alike.

  • Scope: every fixture, the water heater, the main shutoff and any outdoor spigots should be named.
  • Material and brand of pipe and fittings, written out, along with the type of joint.
  • Wall and ceiling repair: who patches and paints, and how far the patching goes.
  • Permits and inspection. Ask the Simsbury building department whether your job needs a permit, and who pulls it.
  • Pressure test: when it happens and who witnesses it.
  • Water shutoff plan: how long you will be without water and what happens to toilets and showers meanwhile.
  • Licensing: check the plumber's Connecticut license in the state eLicense lookup.

Alternatives to a full repipe

Not every leaking house needs new pipe.

Spot repair

A single failed joint or a short section can be cut out and replaced, a sensible fix when the rest of the line is sound.

Partial repipe

Replace the worst run, such as a basement trunk line, and leave the rest.

Epoxy lining

A coating applied inside the pipe. Ask about its drinking water approval, how long it has been used in homes and what it does to flow. Get answers in writing.

Service line work

If the leak is between the street and the house, the fix is water line repair or replacement, not a repipe.

What Simsbury homeowners should know about water suppliers and lead

Simsbury has three public water suppliers. The Town says Aquarion is the provider in most cases and also lists Connecticut Water and the Tariffville Water Commission, and it does not map which streets belong to which. A private well is also possible, and wells fall under the Farmington Valley Health District. The first step is to find your supplier on your bill and ask it about your service line.

EPA says homes with lead service lines usually get most lead from them, and that lead pipes are more likely in homes built before 1986. Under EPA rules, utilities had an initial service line inventory due October 16, 2024, and a baseline inventory is due November 1, 2027. Ask your utility if your line is listed and what material it is. A repipe inside the house does not replace a lead line outside it.

Aquarion's 2025 Simsbury Regional report says the utility treats its water to minimize lead leaching, and it lists lead at the 90th percentile as not detected, with a footnote that one sampled location exceeded the action level. For a home with older fixtures or solder, a lab test of your own tap is the only way to know. Ask your utility, then ask a plumber to look at your fittings.
