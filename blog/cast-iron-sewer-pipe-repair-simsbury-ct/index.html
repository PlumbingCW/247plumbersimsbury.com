Cast Iron Sewer Pipe Repair in Simsbury, CT: How It Fails and What to Do

Written by the Simsbury 24/7 Plumbing team · Published October 15, 2026 · 8 min read

If your drain line is cast iron, rust, cracks and sagging can turn a slow drain into a backup. Here is how the pipe fails, how it is diagnosed and which repairs fit.

Cast iron was a common choice for drain and sewer pipe for decades, and many older homes still have it in the ground under the basement floor or between the house and the street. It is strong and quiet, but it does not last forever. Inside, the metal rusts and builds up scale, and outside, soil and roots work on the joints.

Homeowners usually find out when a drain slows, a toilet gurgles or sewage backs into a floor drain. This guide explains how cast iron fails, how a plumber confirms it and how the repair options compare, so you can ask the right questions before you sign off on a dig.

How cast iron fails from the inside out

Rust is the first problem. Water and waste slowly corrode the inner wall, and flakes and scale build up in rough lumps that narrow the pipe and catch paper and grease. Over time the wall thins, and a thin spot can crack from soil pressure or a heavy load above it.

Joints are the second weak point. Older cast iron was often joined with lead and oakum at hub-and-spigot fittings, and those joints can loosen and open. Small roots find the gap, enter and grow into a mass that snags everything that follows.

A belly is a low spot where the line has sagged. It is usually a soil problem, not a pipe-material problem, and it holds water and waste instead of letting it flow. A belly makes repeated clogs, because solids settle in the dip every time.

Signs the problem is in the sewer line

  • Several drains slow down or gurgle at the same time, not just one fixture.
  • A basement floor drain or a ground-floor toilet backs up when you run water elsewhere.
  • Clogs return within weeks or months after each cleaning.
  • You smell sewer gas in a bathroom or the basement, or notice a damp patch on the lawn.
  • A plumber's snake keeps hitting something hard or comes back with rusty flakes and roots.

Finding the failure with a camera

A sewer camera inspection pushes a small waterproof camera down a cleanout and records what is in the pipe. It shows scale, cracks, offset joints, roots and bellies, and a locator can mark the depth and position of a problem from above ground. You get an actual picture of the pipe instead of a guess.

Cleaning comes before or after the camera, depending on the blockage. Hydro jetting cuts roots and scale off the wall with high-pressure water, but on thin or cracked cast iron it should be judged by the camera first. Jetting a pipe that is already failing can open a crack or wash away the last bit of support.

Homeowners in Granby and Avon who suspect an old line can use the same approach, starting with a recording of the pipe.

Repair options compared

No single method fits every pipe. The camera and the location of the damage decide which one is realistic.

OptionBest whenTrade-offs
Cleaning onlyThe pipe is sound but roots or scale are catching wasteA temporary fix. Roots and scale return unless the opening is repaired
Spot repair with couplingsOne section is cracked, offset or collapsedNeeds digging or floor cutting at that spot, and leaves the rest of the old pipe in place

What Simsbury homeowners should know about sewer, septic and digging

Not every Simsbury property is connected to public sewer. The Town says to call the Water Pollution Control Facility at 860-658-3258 to learn whether a property is in the sewer service area, and septic systems fall under the Farmington Valley Health District at 860-352-2333. Find out which you have before you plan any repair, because a septic line failure is handled differently.

On a sewered property, the Town says the pipes inside the building and the lateral to the main belong to the owner, and that lateral repairs must be done by a licensed plumber. Connecticut statute counts lateral sanitary lines as plumbing work. If sewage is visible outside or in the street, report it to the Water Pollution Control Facility, because it may be a public main problem.

Before any digging, call 811 or 1-800-922-4455. State law requires notice at least two full business days before power-assisted digging, a homeowner doing it has to call, and a contractor must call when one is hired. The service is free and failure to call is the largest single root cause of damage to buried lines nationwide, so build those days into your repair schedule.
